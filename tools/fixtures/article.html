How coffee reached Istanbul

By Ayşe Demir · 6 min read

Coffee reached Istanbul in the middle of the sixteenth century, carried north by merchants who had tasted it in Yemen and Egypt. Within a few decades the drink had changed the rhythm of the city: people who had met only in mosques, markets and private homes now had a public room of their own, open from morning until late at night.

The first coffeehouses opened near the Tahtakale market around 1555. Travellers described rooms lined with low benches, a brazier in the middle, and a steady crowd of craftsmen, scholars and poets. Some came for the coffee, many for the conversation, and a few for the games of backgammon and chess that went on for hours.

Preparation was a craft. Beans were roasted in shallow pans, ground to a powder finer than flour, and boiled with water and, when it was available, sugar, in a small long-handled pot. The foam that rose to the top was valued above everything else; a cup served without it was considered a failure of the host.

A public room of their own

Not everyone welcomed the new habit. Religious authorities worried about crowds gathering to talk about the affairs of the state, and more than one sultan ordered the coffeehouses closed. Each ban was eventually lifted, partly because the drink had become too popular to suppress and partly because it brought in taxes.

By the seventeenth century the coffeehouse had become a kind of newspaper. Storytellers performed there in the evenings, letters were read aloud, and news from the provinces travelled faster through its doors than through any official channel. Merchants arranged deals, and neighbours settled disputes over a shared pot.

"Coffee should be black as hell, strong as death and sweet as love."

From Istanbul the custom spread west. Venetian traders brought beans home, and by the end of the century coffeehouses were opening in London, Paris and Vienna, many of them consciously copying the Ottoman rooms that visitors had described. The ritual of the small cup and the long conversation travelled with them.

Today Turkish coffee is recognised by UNESCO as part of the world's intangible cultural heritage. It is still made the old way in many homes: ground fine, boiled slowly, served with a glass of water and something sweet, and followed, if the company is right, by reading the patterns left in the grounds.
